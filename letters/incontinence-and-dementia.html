Incontinence and dementia: toileting without the shame

The Dementia House Call · Letter No. 18

Of all the things families find hard to talk about, this is near the top. The first accident is often the moment a spouse or a daughter quietly thinks, I can’t do this. And for the person living with dementia, it can be the most humiliating part of the illness, if we let it be.

It doesn’t have to be. Much of what gets called incontinence in dementia is something more fixable, and most of the rest can be managed with dignity intact. This letter covers both.

First: is this sudden?

If accidents started suddenly over days, or come with new confusion, fever, pain, or they seem unwell, that is not the dementia progressing. It can be a urinary tract infection (UTI), severe constipation, a new medication, a bladder that isn’t emptying properly, or a delirium. If new loss of bladder or bowel control comes with new leg weakness, numbness, or back pain, or they can’t pass urine at all, don’t wait for a call back: call 911 or go now. A sudden change is medical until proven otherwise — that needs a phone call, not a new brand of pads. See when to call the doctor and when to go.

One caution the other way: strong-smelling or cloudy urine on its own, in someone who seems otherwise well, is not proof of an infection. Older adults often have bacteria in their urine without being ill, and treating that with antibiotics can do more harm than good. Describe what has changed in your person, not just what the urine looks like.

Often it isn’t the bladder. It’s the bathroom.

Many accidents in the middle stage happen because someone can’t find the bathroom in time, doesn’t recognize the toilet, can’t manage the zipper, or doesn’t read the signal from their body as “I need to go” until it’s too late. That is good news, because much of it can be fixed.

  • Make the bathroom findable. Leave the door open and a light on. Put a simple picture sign of a toilet on the door, at their eye level.
  • Make the toilet visible. A white toilet on a white floor can disappear for someone whose brain misreads contrast. A colored toilet seat that stands out against the floor helps. Skip loose mats around the base: they are a trip hazard, and a dark mat can look like a hole in the floor.
  • Make clothes easy. Elastic waists instead of buttons, belts and zippers. Fewer layers.
  • Make it safe to sit and stand. A toilet safety rail gives them something to hold, and a raised seat helps if getting up is the struggle.
  • Watch for the signs. Restlessness, pulling at clothes, standing up and sitting down, or wandering off often means I need the bathroom. Behavior is communication.

Take them before they need to go

Instead of waiting to be asked, offer the bathroom on a regular schedule, often every two to three hours while awake, and at the natural moments: on waking, after meals, before going out, and before bed. Keep a simple log for a few days of when accidents happen. Most people have a pattern, and once you see it, you can get there first.

Say it as an invitation, not a question. “Are you wet?” invites a no and embarrassment. “Come with me, let’s wash our hands before lunch” gets you both to the bathroom.

What makes it worse

  • Cutting back on drinks during the day. It is one of the most common mistakes. Less fluid makes urine more concentrated, which irritates the bladder, and adds constipation and dehydration, both of which make confusion worse. Keep daytime drinks up. It’s reasonable to ease off in the last couple of hours before bed.
  • Caffeine and alcohol, which make the bladder more urgent. Switching to decaf is an easy win.
  • Constipation, which presses on the bladder. It is common, often missed, and worth asking about.
  • Some medications, including diuretics (“water pills”) given late in the day and some sleeping or sedating medicines. Ask for a review, and don’t stop anything on your own.
  • Scolding, sighing, or the look. They may not remember the accident, but they will remember feeling ashamed, and shame makes people hide wet clothes and refuse help.

Products: what actually helps

Call them underwear. Whatever you buy, the word matters. “Diapers” tells an adult they have become a baby.

For light leaks, washable leakproof underwear can look and feel like ordinary underwear, which makes it easier for someone to accept. EverLeakProof (paid link) makes styles for women and boxers for men. It suits light to moderate bladder leaks, not full loss of bladder or bowel control. It may suit you, too: bladder leaks are common in women, caregivers included, and rarely talked about.

For heavier incontinence, disposable pull-up underwear is easier than tape-on briefs for someone who still walks to the bathroom. Use an overnight absorbency at night, such as LivDry overnight underwear (paid link), so neither of you is changing sheets at 3 a.m.

Protect the bed and the favorite chair with disposable underpads (paid link), or a washable waterproof mattress protector.

Protect the skin. Urine left on skin breaks it down quickly. Change promptly, clean gently with a no-rinse perineal cleanser (paid link) rather than scrubbing with soap, pat dry, and use a barrier cream. Check the skin daily. Redness that doesn’t fade, broken skin, or a sore needs to be seen by a nurse or doctor, not treated at home.

Incontinence at night

A lit route from bed to bathroom, a bedside commode if the bathroom is far, and overnight underwear cover most nights. If they are up many times a night, mention it. There may be a treatable reason, from swollen ankles to an enlarged prostate to the timing of a pill. And the nighttime trip to the bathroom is one of the most common times to fall; the falls letter covers the alarms that let you know they are up.

Borrow this sentence

“That happens. Let’s get you comfortable.” Said in an ordinary voice, as if it were spilled tea.

One line for the log: the time of each accident, and what happened just before.

And for you

Incontinence is one of the most common reasons families feel home care has become impossible, and it is a legitimate reason. If you are changing sheets every night, lifting someone who can’t help, or your own back or sleep is breaking down, that is not a failure of love. It is a sign the load has outgrown one person, and it belongs in the conversation in is it time for long-term care? Before then, ask about home care help with bathing and toileting. It is one of the most useful kinds of help to bring in.
