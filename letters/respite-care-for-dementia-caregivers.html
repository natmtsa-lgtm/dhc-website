For you, the caregiver

What respite actually is, and how to ask for it

The Dementia House Call · Letter No. 19

“I can’t leave him.” I hear it on almost every house call, usually from someone who hasn’t had an unbroken night’s sleep in a year. It is said as a fact, the way you’d say the roof leaks. And it is almost never true. What is true is that nobody has explained what respite actually is, who pays for it, or how to ask for it in a way that gets a yes.

So this letter does that. It is the one I wish I could hand over at the kitchen table.

Respite is a shift change, not a vacation

The word puts people off. It sounds like a spa weekend, and you don’t want a spa weekend. You want to go to the dentist. You want to sleep through one night. You want to go to your own grandchild’s game without your phone in your hand.

That is respite. It is any arrangement where someone else takes the shift so you can put the job down for a few hours, a day, or a week. Any job with no shifts, no weekends and no backup eventually breaks the worker. Caregiving is no different. The families who last are not the ones who never need a break. They are the ones who build the break in before they are desperate.

One more thing, because guilt is the reason most people don’t read past this paragraph: your person does not need you to be there every hour. They need you to still be standing next year. Sustained caregiver exhaustion is one of the strongest predictors of a person with dementia moving into care earlier than they otherwise would. Taking the break is how you keep them home longer.

The four kinds of respite

  • Someone comes to you. A home care aide from an agency, a trained volunteer, a paid neighbor, or a family member, for a few hours at a time. Your person stays in their own chair with their own routine. This is the easiest kind to start with and the easiest for them to accept.
  • Adult day programs. Your person goes out for the day, usually with a meal, activities, and staff who know dementia, and comes home in the afternoon. Many families are surprised how well it goes: a room full of people, music, and someone else asking the questions. Some programs offer transport. Two or three days a week gives you back real, repeating time.
  • A short stay somewhere else. Many assisted living and memory care communities, and some nursing homes, take short-term respite residents, usually for a week to a month, sometimes longer. This is how you go to a wedding, have your own surgery, or simply sleep for a week. It is also a low-stakes way to see how a community looks after your person before any bigger decision.
  • The respite you don’t call respite. A sister who takes every Sunday afternoon. A church member who sits with them on Thursday mornings. A neighbor who does the pharmacy run. It counts. It usually only happens when you ask for a specific job on a specific day.

Who pays

This is the part that stops most families, so here is the plain version for the United States. Details vary by state and change over time, so treat this as a map, not a contract.

  • Original Medicare does not cover ordinary respite. A few Medicare Advantage plans add limited in-home support or caregiver benefits, so if your person has one, call the plan and ask. The exception that applies to everyone is hospice: once your person is on the Medicare hospice benefit, it covers short inpatient respite stays of up to five days at a time in an approved facility, and that can be used more than once. If they are on hospice, ask the hospice team about it directly. It is an underused benefit.
  • Medicaid covers respite in many states through home and community-based services waivers, for people who qualify financially and by level of care. Waiting lists are common, so apply before you need it.
  • Veterans. If your person is a veteran enrolled in VA health care, the VA offers respite, at home, in adult day programs, or as short stays, generally up to 30 days a year. Ask their VA social worker.
  • Your Area Agency on Aging. Every part of the country has one, funded under the Older Americans Act. The National Family Caregiver Support Program that runs through them can pay for respite hours, and the agency knows every local program you don’t. Find yours through the Eldercare Locator at eldercare.acl.gov or 1-800-677-1116.
  • Long-term care insurance, if your person has a policy, usually includes a respite benefit. Read the policy or call the insurer and ask.
  • Grants and nonprofits. Some Alzheimer’s and caregiver organizations offer respite grants or a number of free care hours. The Alzheimer’s Association helpline, 1-800-272-3900, is open 24 hours a day and can tell you what exists where you live. The ARCH National Respite Locator lists programs by state.
  • Paying yourself. Adult day programs are usually the best value. In the 2024 and 2025 cost surveys, a day at a program usually cost a fraction of what the same hours from a home aide cost. Private home care is billed by the hour, and short-term community stays by the day. Ask what is included before you compare.

How to ask, so you get a yes

Ask your own doctor. At your appointment, not theirs, say the sentence from the running-on-empty letter: “I’m a caregiver, and I’m running on empty.” Then add: “Who do I call about respite?” A social worker referral is often one sentence away.

Ask the Area Agency on Aging in these words: “I’m caring for my mother, who has dementia, at home. What respite programs are there in this county, and what do I have to do to qualify?” Write down the name of the person you spoke to.

Ask family for a job, not for help. “Let me know if you can ever help” gets nothing, because it asks them to design the help. Try: “Could you take Dad every Sunday from one to five? I’ll leave everything ready. I need to know that time is mine.” Give the job, the day, and the hours. A brother who lives far away can take the phone calls, the insurance, and the pharmacy instead.

Ask a community for a trial stay before you need one. “Do you take respite residents, what is the minimum stay, and what would you need from me to be ready?” Do this in a calm month, so that the week you have surgery is not also the week you start phoning around.

Making it go well

  • Start small and repeat. Two hours, the same person, the same day each week. Familiarity is what your person can still learn.
  • Don’t ask their permission. Tell them who is coming. “Would you like someone to come and sit with you?” gets a no. “Marie is coming Thursday to help me with the house, and she’ll have lunch with you.” A helper introduced as a friend, a housekeeper, or a volunteer from the church is easier to accept than one introduced as a caregiver.
  • Hand over the sheet. Whoever takes the shift needs the routine, the medication list, what calms your person, what upsets them, and who to call. If you have the one page you hand the paramedics, you have most of it already. For a short stay, the community will need the medication list and recent medical history in writing, and it helps to send the familiar blanket, the photographs, and the music.
  • Expect the first few visits to be bumpy. Some agitation, some “Who is that woman?”, some protest when you leave. That is expected, and it usually settles over the first few visits. Don’t cancel after the first one.
  • Actually leave. Hovering in the next room teaches everyone, including you, that the arrangement doesn’t hold. Go out. Sleep. Sit in the car. The point is that the shift is not yours.

When it isn’t just settling in

A little unsettledness with a new person or a new place is normal. A sudden change is not. If your person comes home from a day program or a short stay noticeably more confused, drowsy, agitated, or unsteady than they went in, treat it as medical until proven otherwise. The same goes if the change happens over hours or days rather than weeks. That is a delirium until someone shows you it isn’t, and delirium has causes: an infection, a new medication, constipation, pain, dehydration, a fall nobody mentioned. It needs a phone call, not a note that the respite “didn’t suit them.” See when to call the doctor, and when to go.

Borrow this sentence

For the guilt, which will come: “I am taking a break so that I can keep doing this.” Say it to yourself, and to the relative who raises an eyebrow.

One line for the log: the date of each respite session, who came, and how your person was that evening. After a month you will be able to see whether it is working, and you will have the record that programs and waivers ask for.

And for you

If reading this made your throat tighten, that is information. It usually means the break is overdue, not that it is undeserved. Make one call this week: your doctor, or the Eldercare Locator. One. Then let the next step come from that.
