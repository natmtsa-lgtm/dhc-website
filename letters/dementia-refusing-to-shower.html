When your person with dementia refuses to shower

The Dementia House Call · Letter No. 22

The fight usually goes like this. You say, “Dad, you need a shower.” He says he had one this morning. He didn’t — it has been nine days. You say so. Now the two of you are standing in the hallway, arguing about a fact that one of you can no longer hold on to.

Here is what I’d want you to know first: the refusal is rarely about being clean. Look at a shower from their side. A cold room. Being undressed by someone else — sometimes by their own child. Water hitting the face without warning. A slippery floor and a body that doesn’t feel steady anymore. And a string of steps — undress, step in, soap, rinse, dry — that the brain can no longer put in order. “No” is often the only word left for all of that. Behavior is communication.

Change the room before you change their mind

Warm the bathroom, and lay out the towels and clean clothes before you invite them in. A handheld shower head and a shower chair turn a frightening spray into something you can aim: start at the feet and work up, and leave the hair and face for last — or for another day, at the sink. Keep them covered. A towel over the shoulders or lap, with you washing underneath it, protects both warmth and modesty. Put a washcloth in their hand and give them a job: “Can you do your arms?”

For safety: grab bars, a non-slip mat, and your own hand testing the water, because they may no longer judge hot from warm. Don’t leave them alone in the tub or shower.

Change the words

“Do you want a shower?” invites a no. Try an invitation with the next step built in: “The bathroom’s nice and warm. Come with me.” Go with the time of day they washed for fifty years; a lifelong evening bather often won’t welcome a 9 a.m. shower. And if the answer is no, don’t argue the facts. Step away, and try again in twenty minutes — sometimes with a different reason: “Let’s freshen up before lunch.”

Less than you think is enough

A full shower every day is a habit, not a medical need. For most people with dementia, two or three times a week is plenty, with a wash at the sink in between: face, hands, underarms, feet, and private areas. If there is incontinence, the private areas need cleaning each time.

And a shower isn’t the only way. A towel bath — warm, damp towels and a no-rinse soap, one part of the body at a time, the rest kept covered, in a chair or in bed — gets a person clean. In a nursing-home trial, it led to less agitation and aggression than usual bathing care — and so did a gentler, person-centered shower.

When it isn’t just a no

If someone who bathed willingly last week now refuses outright, or cries out or guards a limb when you help them move, think pain, infection, or delirium. A sudden change is medical until proven otherwise — that is a call to the doctor, not a behavior strategy. And while you’re helping, glance at the skin: redness or a rash is worth mentioning at the next appointment, and an open sore is worth a call sooner.

The permission slip

You’re allowed to skip the shower today. Clean enough, with the two of you still on speaking terms, beats spotless and shaken. Try again tomorrow.

From my kitchen table to yours,
